About

Who this is, and why this archive exists.

I am Ankush Sarkar. I have spent quite a while between learning, making, collecting, and building, sometimes for an audience, but more often simply because I wanted to understand something, solve a problem, or create something. This archive gathers those things in one place: the work, the tools, the interests, the me.

"Jack of all trades, master of none, though oftentimes better than the master of one."

A.

Content

The start of my content creation journey.

It began around 2019–2020, when gaming content was everywhere and I wanted to try making some of it myself. A Minecraft Survival Let's Play was one of the first things I recorded, around version 1.14. I also played Happy Wheels, which also became a recurring series, and Pokémon X/Y through Citra Emulator. The setup was not exactly designed for recording: a Pentium processor, 4 GB of RAM, and a hard drive were being asked to run a game, recording software, and the operating system simultaneously.

A lot of the process was experimentation. OBS was one of the tools I used, but I also tried other recording software, including Windows Game Bar, Bandicam, Camtasia and Icecream Screen Recorder, largely because the limiting factor was often simply how much the computer could take. Some games were playable; recording them was a different beast entirely. I also played Tekken 3 and some older Hitman games, although those were never recorded.

There was another recording setup that was considerably less conventional.

My father had a Samsung Galaxy J2 with 1 GB of RAM and 8 GB of storage. When I wanted to record something like Happy Wheels, I would sometimes wait until he came home around 9 or 10 at night, quickly take the phone, find a quiet corner of the house, and try to record without anyone noticing. It was partly because I didn't want to be embarrassed about what I was doing, and partly because having other people around made recording difficult. Sometimes they would talk loudly enough for their voices to end up in the recording anyway.

It was primitive, occasionally ridiculous, and technically rather painful. But it was the first time I was actually trying to make something and put it in front of other people.

The channel is gone now. What remains is mostly the memory of the process itself — including the hardware that was being pushed far beyond what it was really meant to do.

Starting as a collaborative project with my neighbour-cum-elder-brother, Subhabrata Ghosh, on 13 August 2020, the idea was different from Timekeeper.

It was about commentary. We talked about things happening around us — COVID, gaming and app bans, and whatever else seemed worth discussing. The style was influenced in part by the social-commentary and reaction videos I was watching at the time, including videos by Triggered Insaan.

It was also an experiment in making something together. The channel eventually became inactive, but it was never erased. The channel still exists, which means that unlike some of my earlier work, this particular piece of history still has a surviving public record.

I don't need to reconstruct everything that was ever uploaded here. The channel itself is the archive.

This was where I moved heavily into anime editing and began treating the editing process itself as something worth learning. Created on 22 January 2022, this channel's run was essentially a sine curve over the interval [0, 2π].

I spent a substantial amount of time experimenting with After Effects, effects, plugins, rendering, and the general process of turning an idea into an anime music video. The machine doing all of this was still the same sort of hardware that had struggled with much simpler things, so rendering could take hours, even full nights.

I experimented constantly — not just with the videos themselves, but with effects, thumbnails, and different ways of presenting the finished work.

One of the videos that mattered most was a Madara AMV.

I remember waking up one day and discovering that the video had reached roughly 200,000 views. It wasn't tagged as a Short, nor was it within the acceptable duration for one. Shorts were still relatively early at the time, and classification depended much more heavily on tagging. I had somehow managed to reach that audience through the ordinary pool of videos rather than the Shorts feed.

Then came a completely different kind of validation.

One day I opened Instagram and found that someone had re-uploaded the video. My first reaction was fury. Then I noticed that they had credited me and mentioned my Instagram account.

The sheer happiness that sixteen-year-old me felt is difficult to put into words.

Someone had found something I had made, liked it enough to share it, and thought it was worth identifying the person behind it. For a sixteen-year-old making anime edits on a struggling computer, that felt enormous.

And then the channel disappeared.

Sometime between January and March 2023, I ran into an account-access problem involving Google's date-of-birth verification. I had been using my father's email while the account itself used my name, and changes to Google's security and verification requirements apparently prevented me from logging back in. I still don't know with certainty whether I deleted the account myself or whether it was deleted automatically. What I know is that the channel was suddenly gone.

The loss did more than hurt. It made me afraid of creating again.

'What if I achieve my goals and it is snatched away from me in an instant?'

That thought stayed with me.

It reminded me of another loss from that period: a Pokémon game my father had deleted after I had spent a long time playing it and building up its progress. The comparison sounds disproportionate from the outside, but the feeling was similar: something I had invested an enormous amount of myself into was suddenly no longer there.

The channel is gone. A handful of screenshots remain.

So this entry is necessarily incomplete — but perhaps that's appropriate. ASenpai is one of those parts of the archive where the surviving fragments are themselves part of the story.

The channel that eventually became my current home on YouTube did not begin with confidence.

After losing ASenpai, I was genuinely afraid to start again. I had seen what it felt like to build something, put time into it, watch it reach people, and then lose the entire thing in an instant. For quite a while, I simply didn't have the courage to open another channel.

Eventually, I did.

The beginning was deliberately low-stakes. I posted anime clips because I didn't want to invest much into something that I was still afraid of losing. That approach didn't last particularly well; I ended up receiving a copyright claim, which was probably a fairly efficient way of learning that "low effort" does not necessarily mean "low consequence."

I moved toward science videos after that, putting considerably more effort into what I made. Then came another experimental phase: low-effort Shorts covering science, psychology, and my "YouTube Automation Phase".

Eventually, I stopped trying to figure out what would be easiest to make and settled into what I actually wanted to make: proper self-improvement and self-help videos.

This is now the channel I continue to build.

Beyond The Boundaries was supposed to be an intellectual discussion hub.

The idea was to have conversations that could move across philosophy, literature, humanity, science and whatever else was worth thinking about, not necessarily fitting everything into one narrow subject, but exploring ideas for the sake of exploring them.

The idea came about during a random conversation with Soumyadeep Das. At one point, we actually tried to record an episode. It was at night, and the practical realities of being two people living with their families quickly became apparent: people were calling us for dinner, interruptions kept happening, and even the internet situation of the time made recording considerably more difficult than it needed to be.

We never actually published anything.

And yet the channel still exists.

So Beyond The Boundaries occupies a slightly strange place in the archive: it is a project that never quite became a public work, but the idea itself was real, the attempt was real, and the channel remains as evidence that we intended to build it.

Ankush's Substack began as a newsletter connected to my YouTube work. It stopped, and eventually I started it again.

The basic idea is simple: I live my life, things happen, I think about them, and then I inevitably start trying to connect them to everything else.

I write about day-to-day events, but the writing is rarely just a record of what happened. I tend to derive philosophical frameworks from ordinary experiences, to take something that happened during the day and ask what it says about something larger.

The writing is mood-based. I don't sit down with a perfectly predetermined publishing schedule and manufacture a topic because the calendar says I should. A daily rant can suddenly become a post when I realise that it connects several things I have been thinking about.

That tendency toward connection is probably the most consistent feature of the whole thing.

I have always been somewhat obsessed with coherence, taking apparently separate observations, domains, ideas and experiences and trying to understand how they fit together. The Substack is where that process becomes writing.

B.

Science & Ideas

I started thinking about this when I was 17.

A late-night in early September, a lot of backlogs, and I was doing what I apparently deemed a reasonable use of my time while preparing for the Joint Entrance Exam: trying to understand the fundamental nature of consciousness and the universe.

I tend to approach problems by trying to find contradictions. If an idea seems incomplete, I try to push it until something breaks, and then see whether the contradiction tells me something useful.

That eventually led me to a much bigger question:

What if consciousness isn't something that suddenly appears when a sufficiently complicated brain is assembled?

What if it exists on a spectrum?

That became the starting point for CTIC.

The central idea is that consciousness is not necessarily binary. A human being isn't simply conscious while a rock is simply not conscious. Instead, I proposed a quantity I called Φ (Phi): an intrinsic measure that could exist to some degree in everything.

That immediately creates a rather strange implication.

A rock could have a very small Φ. A human brain could have a much larger one. But that doesn't mean the human is simply "more conscious" because it has more atoms. The structure and interaction of those components would matter too. I even started thinking about whether different elements could have different weights in this hypothetical scalar, with carbon receiving particular attention because of the enormous variety of structures it can form.

That also led me into one of the ideas I found most interesting: consciousness and intelligence might not be the same thing at all.

We tend to define intelligence from our own perspective. An ant could have an entirely different definition of what constitutes intelligence. A bed-sheet cannot solve calculus, but that doesn't necessarily tell us anything about whether it could possess some extremely low level of Φ.

From there, the theory started spreading into other parts of physics.

I became particularly interested in entropy.

Instead of thinking about entropy simply as disorder increasing, I began exploring the idea of entropy as a form of optimization or informational efficiency. A floppy disk and an SD card can both store information, but one achieves enormously greater information density and efficiency than the other. That became one of the analogies I used to think about what "higher entropy" might mean in the framework I was building.

Then quantum mechanics got involved, because apparently one foundational problem wasn't enough.

I started thinking about Feynman's path-integral formulation, where a system can be described through contributions from many possible paths. I played with the idea that the trajectories we observe could somehow emerge from the interaction of extreme possibilities. At one point I even imagined an electron taking an absurd journey across the universe and ending up on a friend's face, simply because it was a useful way of visualizing how wildly different possible paths could exist mathematically even when almost all of them contribute negligibly to what we actually observe.

The theory eventually became cosmological as well.

If everything possesses Φ, and if the universe itself can be thought of in terms of this spectrum, then the beginning and end of the universe become interesting in a completely different way. I explored a cyclic picture in which the universe eventually returns toward an extremely compact, highly efficient state, connecting the beginning and end through the same fundamental condition.

At that point, CTIC had stopped being a theory about consciousness in isolation.

It had become an attempt to unify consciousness, information, entropy, quantum mechanics and cosmology under one conceptual framework.

That was also the part I found most interesting: the possibility that what we call physics, chemistry and biology are not fundamentally separate worlds, but different ways of looking at the same underlying events. A falling apple can simultaneously be a problem in gravitational physics, a chemical interaction with its environment, and a biological object undergoing decay. The disciplines change; the event does not.

The theory is raw. It is incomplete. I have not yet formalised a mathematical structure or assigned weights to the elements or conducted experiment to measure phi. Some of its assumptions are highly speculative, and it has not been established experimentally. But that wasn't really the point.

The interesting part was seeing how far one initial question could be pushed: from what is consciousness? to what if consciousness is continuous?, then to matter, entropy, information, quantum mechanics and eventually the universe itself.

CTIC is my attempt, at least in its current form, to make those connections coherent.

I have always found The Almighty, Yhwach's ability in Bleach, slightly unsatisfying when taken literally.

The idea is simple enough, as described by Tite Kubo, the author of Bleach: he can see all possible futures and alter them. But if you take that description completely seriously, the ability very quickly stops being interesting. If every possible future is visible and any of them can simply be selected, then there is effectively no uncertainty left, no meaningful strategy, and very little reason for a conflict to exist in the first place.

And spoiler alert, the Still Silver Arrow, then, effectively becomes what I call BWBO [Bad writing to bail out].

So I tried asking a different question:

What if The Almighty were not omniscience, but an extremely powerful computational process?

Instead of imagining every possible future as something Yhwach can simply read simultaneously, I treated the future as an enormous branching state space. Every possible evolution of the universe becomes another trajectory through that space.

The problem is obvious: there are far too many of them. Or as a cursed mathematician would write: ∞=∞

Even if the future is deterministic, that does not mean that calculating every possible future is computationally feasible. So I introduced a constraint: The Almighty has finite computational resources and can only examine a limited portion of the available future space at any given moment.

That changes the nature of the ability considerably.

Yhwach is no longer literally seeing everything. He is searching through an effectively infinite set of possibilities, using heuristics to discard futures that are redundant, irrelevant, or strategically useless. What looks like omniscience from the outside is therefore the result of an absurdly powerful optimization process operating within finite limits.

This also gives the ability something it otherwise lacks: a cost to changing the future.

A future in which Yhwach wins through an ordinary sequence of events should be much easier to reach than one requiring an absurd chain of microscopic coincidences or enormous alterations to causality. The model therefore treats The Almighty as searching for the most useful future that it can actually discover and manipulate, rather than simply picking whatever reality would be convenient. Essentially, rather than picking a future where all the Soul-Reapers are automatically killed, and yes by the Infinite-Monkey Theorem it is indeed possible, Yhwach chooses a mere scratch on the face of the opponent he is currently facing since that's computationally easier to reach.

That led to another interesting question: what happens when somebody interferes with the information being fed into the system?

I tried to reinterpret some of the other abilities in Bleach through the same framework.

Kyōka Suigetsu can be thought of as corrupting the initial state that The Almighty is working from. Ichimonji is even more interesting because it interferes with the representation and identity of things within that state. The Still Silver Arrow can instead be interpreted as temporarily suppressing the future-altering process itself, forcing Yhwach to operate without the mechanism he normally relies upon, though personally it's still BWBO.

None of these are meant to be claims about the actual physics of the Bleach universe. They are attempts to see whether an ability that sounds like unrestricted omnipotence can be made computationally coherent without completely destroying the strategic structure of the story.

The result is a rather different interpretation of The Almighty.

Not omnipotence.

Not merely precognition.

Something closer to an algorithmic approximation of optimal causality.

The full work develops the mathematical framework behind that idea, including the future-state space, computational constraints, search strategy and optimization costs.

This is ultimately a mathematical exercise built around a fictional ability, but it was also an interesting way of asking a more general question:

If something could genuinely search through possible futures and alter which one becomes reality, what would that process actually have to look like?

Why move through space if the distance itself can be reduced?

That was the question I started with in a random summer afternoon of 2026.

The basic idea was: Space-time's effective expansion rate is not bounded by the universal speed limit of 'c' so what if that idea could be reversed and the space between two distant systems could somehow be made to contract. The spacecraft wouldn't need to cross the original distance. The distance itself would change.

I started with Hubble's law and simply asked what its contraction analogue would look like. A lot of calculus later the immediate problem was that the ordinary cosmological contraction rate came out to be ridiculously slow. Using the measured Hubble constant, getting even relatively nearby stars to meaningfully approach each other would take timescales vastly beyond anything useful for travel.

So I tried to make the effect stronger.

One of the first ideas was to make the contraction rate depend on a ratio of the mass of a system to that of its components. That seemed reasonable at first: if you want to engineer an effect, mass is a good starting point.

It went catastrophically wrong.

The scaling meant that sufficiently small objects could become far more powerful "space contractors" than enormous astronomical systems. At one point, a proton would effectively be a better space-time contractor than a galaxy. That was obviously not a particularly promising description of reality. I tried to fix these distortions but to no luck and so those formulations were thrown away.

That led to a more interesting change in the model.

I stopped assuming that the contraction equation should describe what ordinary matter does all the time. Instead, I essentially assumed a hypothetical mechanism that would 'turn on' the that contracting behaviour and this distinction is important. Because the Hubble constant doesn't need to change. Ordinary matter doesn't suddenly start contracting the universe around itself. The hypothetical mechanism is what switches the engineered behaviour on. Once it is switched off, ordinary cosmology is recovered.

From there, the model became less about trying to invent a magical new constant and more about asking a narrower question:

If such an activation mechanism existed, what mathematics would describe the resulting contraction?

I explored several formulations, including a later time relation that behaves differently from the simple exponential contraction model. But that relation is currently a postulate rather than something derived from deeper gravitational physics.

And that is where the model currently stands.

It is not a theory of faster-than-light travel. It is not a claim that space-time can actually be contracted in this way. It is a toy phenomenological model built around a deliberately speculative assumption: suppose some unknown mechanism could temporarily engineer space-time.

The real question is whether any physically consistent stress-energy configuration, or perhaps some extension of gravitational physics, could produce anything remotely resembling the space-time evolution being assumed here.

Projects

I have always preferred owning my music rather than streaming it.

There is something about having the actual file sitting there, knowing exactly what it is, where it came from, and how it is organized, that I prefer over depending on a service to decide what exists in my library.

The collection has gone through a few phases. I started out downloading MP3s, mostly from YouTube and other websites back in 2018, without thinking too much about how they were stored, at least by my standards. Though even then, I hated the inconsistent file names or the name of websites in the file so regardless of the size of the library, I cleaned the file names by hand. It was so odd to see a fellow neighbour's 'Music' folder which had everything in one folder and horrible naming. Then I delved a bit into audio and discovered WAV, FLAC, Opus etc. At one point I deleted a few hundred old files and downloaded the better versions again, one by one.

That was when this stopped being "few folders of music" and became an actual project.

I've spent well over a hundred hours organizing the library and interacting with individual files. At this point I know the roughly 850 files by hand because, at some point, I've probably had to touch their metadata, rename them, reorganize them, or otherwise do something to them.

And I have some rather specific ideas about metadata.

The basic rule is that one thing should do one thing.

If the filename already tells me the title, I don't need the title duplicated in the metadata. If the album is already represented by the folder structure, I don't need another redundant copy of that information floating around unnecessarily. The artist belongs in the artist tag. The folder structure handles the grouping. The filename handles the title.

I even use the composer field differently from how a conventional music library might: for anime music, it can represent the anime or show the music came from.

This might sound unnecessarily obsessive and it probably is.

But there is a reason behind it. I like systems where every piece of information has a clear purpose and where changing one thing doesn't create five redundant versions of the same thing somewhere else.

That philosophy eventually led me to build Python tools around the collection, alongside software such as MusicBrainz Picard and various utilities for searching, cleaning and organizing the files. Eventually I decided that all of this should live in one coherent place, so the project became a GitHub archive as well.

My current audio setup is:

Devices:

- In Ear Monitor [IEM]: Salnotes 7Hz Zero

- Digital To Analogue Converter [DAC]: Audiocular D10

Softwares:

- Laptop: Fooyin/Foobar2000 [Depending on Linux/Windows]

- Phone: Poweramp Music Player [Paid, Full Version]

The folder structure is therefore part of the project itself. It isn't just where the files happen to be stored; it is part of how I think about the collection.

And the music itself is probably less organized by genre or artist than you might expect.

I don't really listen to albums religiously, and I don't have a favourite artist whose entire catalogue I automatically like. A song stays because it resonates with me, whether that's because of the music, the lyrics, or simply something about the way it feels.

Popularity isn't particularly relevant either. A song can be obscure and end up in my collection, or be enormously popular and still belong there. The only real requirement is that I think it's good.

What has emerged is a rather strange overlap of everything I've encountered and liked, filtered through the level of quality and organization I expect from the archive.

This is my music collection, but it is also a small experiment in how I think information should be organized.

Products

Prioritas started with a problem I know rather well: having too many things worth doing.

Technically, this isn't even my first attempt at making something around the idea of focus. The first version was about focusing on something, but I eventually scrapped it because I realized that the problem was larger than simply telling someone to focus.

At one point, I had a list of all the different areas of my life I was trying to work on simultaneously.

And I was trying to work on all of them.

That was the problem.

There is a particular kind of trap that comes with having a lot of interests. You can have ten things that are genuinely important, ten things that you genuinely want to learn, and ten things that you genuinely believe you should be doing. The problem is that they cannot all receive your full attention at the same time.

Eventually I realized that sometimes you have to deliberately abandon the 80%-good option so that you can concentrate on the thing that matters most.

That became the idea behind Prioritas.

The project is an attempt to turn that realization into something practical: a framework for deciding what deserves your attention when there are more good options than you can possibly pursue.

It is particularly relevant to people who constantly find themselves pulled between different ambitions, projects, interests and responsibilities. Being capable of doing many things does not automatically mean doing all of them is a good idea.

Sometimes the hardest decision isn't deciding what to do.

It's deciding what not to do.

Prioritas is my attempt to build something around that problem.

It currently exists as a project in development rather than a finished product. I haven't turned it into the polished course I eventually want it to become, but the underlying idea is one I've personally had to confront repeatedly.

The name is intentional too.

Prioritas is built around the idea of priority in the singular: not an endless list of equally important things, but the thing that deserves to come first.

The Digital Infrastructure

This machine has had the taste of more operating systems than there are planets in our solar system.

Windows

Windows 8.1 The original operating system the machine shipped with.
Windows 10 A reliable operating system that I used for the majority of my past. It was installed by a neighbourhood elder brother when I wasn't really familiar with technology much.

The Technical Optimist

I wanted the full-resolution Planck CMB image. Just as an average person desires on a random Tuesday morning, right? Well, I guess some humans still don't understand why this is cool, so let me put it simply: we have a temperature map of our UNIVERSE when it was JUST 380,000 YEARS OLD. If you don't find that cool, I'm afraid you might be too simple-minded to understand the result of the craziness of some monkeys on a rock.

The ESA website had an image available for download, but I noticed that the version being served wasn't the full-resolution image I was looking for. The site mentioned emailing ESA to get the full-resolution version, so I did.

Due to the site undergoing development, the image Stefan Luders from ESA redirected me to was the same image already displayed on their website. I pointed this out to him, and he explained that it might simply be an issue with ESA Science revamping its website, then pointed me toward the underlying Planck scientific data instead.

So I stopped looking for the image and decided to make it myself.

I spent essentially an entire day experimenting with the data and adjusting the processing and filtering until I got an image I was happy with.

I had to learn Astropy, the Python scientific-data stack, and several other libraries along the way just to get a reasonable-looking image. The entire process ended up teaching me how scientific data is actually processed.

My purist philosophy applied to the data resulted in a pretty good custom filter, an acceptable threshold and, most proudly, a locally generated image of the Cosmic Microwave Background made by my own hands.

I have tremendous respect for you if you understood even half the jargon.

I also have a habit of downloading original astronomical images whenever I find them, even when the files are hundreds of megabytes or occasionally several gigabytes. Apparently liking space images wasn't enough; I wanted the original files too. Also, turns out normal picture viewers are definitely not capable of displaying such large images.

For the majority of my laptop's life, 720p YouTube at 1× was noticeably lagging.

Only because I never sat down to question it.

Until I did.

I could have accepted that the machine was simply too old.

Instead, I started digging through the software stack.

Browser Browser configuration and internals — chrome://flags on Chromium-based browsers, about:config in Firefox, and everything around them.
Video playback Playback behaviour, H.264 vs H.265, AV1 and other video encodings.
Hardware decoding Intel VA-API support, which I was able to investigate in much greater depth after moving to Linux.
Graphics Graphics acceleration and driver behaviour.
Vulkan The machine's partial/incomplete Vulkan support and what could actually be made useful.

Eventually I found that the machine had partial/incomplete Vulkan support alongside usable hardware video decoding.

And I'd like to emphasize: this was NOT "tech idiot forgets to turn on 'Use graphics acceleration when available' in the browser's Performance menu," nor was it simply flipping a browser flag.

It was about finding out that technology had already started to forsake the old graphics driver, yet the graphics driver had not forsaken me yet.

720p YouTube at 1× → noticeable lag

became

1080p YouTube at 2× → smooth

And this was YouTube streaming, not a local video file.

It's amazing how far asking questions like "Why?" can take you.

I was initially pretty sure that running a Large Language Model locally was a terrible idea, especially with only 4 GB of RAM.

After the RAM upgrade, though, the possibility seemed worth a shot.

Ollama My first easy route into local LLMs. It also taught me that the easier something is to set up, the less control you sometimes have over it. I initially got terrible speeds because it wasn't configured properly for my CPU.
Whisper Ran it through Subtitle Edit back in the day. Performance was terrible, so I eventually moved that work to Kaggle.
llama.cpp The important one. It gave me the level of configuration and compilation control I was looking for, and eventually became my first serious encounter with compiling software specifically for my hardware.
Local 8B models Actually ran Qwen's 8B model locally. On this hardware.
Kaggle Used for text-to-speech and text-to-image experimentation. This also introduced me to concepts such as seeds in image generation.

The Kaggle experiments are NOT presented as local hardware achievements. They used remote compute.

8B LLM on a Pentium 3558U

The Pentium 3558U has no AVX and supports SSE4.2.

I compiled llama.cpp specifically against the instruction set available on the CPU.

This was also where I properly encountered compiling software for the first time.

Yes, I know. Any developer reading this probably just thought:

"You're learning compilation while compiling llama.cpp?"

Yes.

I also tested CPU vs GPU usage for LLM-type workloads.

When I was able to make use of the GPU, it was roughly seven times faster than the CPU for these workloads.

The GPU introduced its own compatibility problems because it did not support some of the precision-math requirements expected by the software.

I therefore had to work around those requirements as well.

Qwen 8B initial local performance:

0.1–0.3 tokens per second

After compiling llama.cpp for the actual CPU instruction set and getting GPU usage working:

roughly 0.9–2 tokens per second

Not fast.

But it worked.

And that was the point.

I didn't need the Pentium to be good at running an 8B model. I wanted to know whether I could make it run one at all.

The Personal Catalogue

The DVD drive has somehow survived almost everything that has happened to this laptop.

At one point, an actual ant colony decided that the DVD-drive area was a perfectly reasonable place to live.

I opened the laptop with what I'll describe as a sub-optimal screwdriver, cleaned it, tinkered with it, and somehow it survived that too.

Then, one day, I found an old DVD containing my father's wedding footage. And for the first time ever, I actually thought "Oh, thank goodness I have a DVD-drive" and I copied and preserved the footage.

The drive then had another life. After I upgraded the laptop from its original hard drive to an SSD, I reused the DVD-drive bay as a caddy for the old hard drive and then, in August 2026, that hard drive finally failed.

The DVD drive, meanwhile, still works.

"Somehow, after everything, this machine keeps finding another reason not to be thrown away."

This laptop has spent more than a decade being repaired, upgraded, repurposed and kept alive. It has gone from its original hard drive, through an SSD upgrade, to becoming a storage machine, a creative workstation, a scientific-computing machine and whatever label you put on it after reading through the entire website. Yes, I like 4th-Wall breaks.

The technology changed, the machine stayed, and I kept finding things for it to do.

I have a somewhat obsessive relationship with keeping things organized.

Not because I particularly enjoy making folders for the sake of making folders, but because I dislike the idea of things existing in several messy, disconnected places when they could have one proper home.

I discovered this quite clearly when I once went through Subhabrata Ghosh's mixed music folder as I mentioned above.

Everything was just... there.

Different artists, albums, songs and files all mixed together.

I hated it. Personally, of course.

Not because the files themselves were wrong, though they were because they were just random MP3 encodes from random websites, but because the structure was.

I prefer things to be classified properly, kept together when they belong together, and separated when they don't.

That philosophy has gradually turned my own digital storage into something resembling a personal archive.

As of now, I've roughly sorted 500 directories across around 10,000 files, covering things like academics, school life, personal media, documents, writing, photographs, videos, projects and other parts of my digital life. By hand.

There is also a principle behind it:

"I don't want redundancy."

"Beware about 4th-wall breaks, you have been warned."

If something already has a proper home, I don't want to create another copy of it somewhere else just because I happen to be building a website.

My music archive belongs with the music project. My writing belongs where it is published. Other material can live in its own appropriate home.

The website can point to those places rather than pretending everything needs to be duplicated here.

That same principle is why I care about source material.

For me, purism means getting as close to the source as possible.

If I can have the original, I want the original.

If the original isn't practical, I want the closest lossless representation.

That is why, for example:

WAV → FLAC → encoded formats

matters to me, while a fake FLAC is essentially an insult disguised as a file extension.

Or how I'd happily spend 60GB getting the proper movie files which people are generally willing to spend 4GB for.

It is also why I care about how things are actually presented. I use mpv because I want good playback without unnecessary visual processing, and I generally prefer seeing material as close as possible to how it was actually created rather than piling enhancement on top of enhancement.

The same instinct appeared in the Planck project. I didn't want the image somebody had already prepared because it was not tuned to what I view as "pure". I wanted the underlying data and the ability to produce the image myself.

And it extends into the things I consume as well.
